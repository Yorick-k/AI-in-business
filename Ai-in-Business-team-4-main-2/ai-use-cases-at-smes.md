## 1. The question, for this kind of SME

*Which AI use is worth trying first in a small business-service firm — from a two-person office to one of about 75 people — where people retype information from e-mails and PDFs into other systems? And will the time saved justify the cost?*

Two sizes, one problem. In a firm of two, the owner usually does the administration, often after hours. In a firm of 30, one or two staff do it. Both run on e-mail, documents, customer data and invoices, and both already use an accounting package and office software. Information is copied, checked and passed on by hand before the next step can start. The owner sees time being lost but cannot say where, or what fixing it would be worth.

This page answers three questions from our research proposal: which repetitive workflow suits AI, what the firm needs to start, and what it could save and cost. For a team of two, time is the scarcest resource, so the page looks for light, low-threshold tools, not systems that need a dedicated team.

## 2. The translation: what firms like yours use AI for

AI is spreading in firms of every size, but the smallest lag behind. In 2025, 14% of Dutch firms with 2–10 people used at least one AI technology, against 27% of firms with 10–50 people. In specialised business services it was 31%, up from 13% in 2023 ([CBS, 2025](https://www.cbs.nl/nl-nl/nieuws/2025/50/bedrijven-gebruiken-ai-vaakst-voor-marketing-of-verkoop)). Microbusinesses that use AI mostly take it ready-made: 48% use commercial software as it comes ([CBS, 2026](https://www.cbs.nl/nl-nl/longread/rapportages/2026/gebruik-van-ai-technologie-door-nederlandse-microbedrijven/samenvatting)).

The most common AI technology is **text mining**: software that reads documents and pulls out the facts. 12% of all Dutch firms used it in 2025, against 4% in 2023 ([CBS, 2025](https://www.cbs.nl/nl-nl/nieuws/2025/50/bedrijven-gebruiken-ai-vaakst-voor-marketing-of-verkoop)). ### What pays off — and what we could not find

The evidence that AI pays off for small firms is mostly what firms *say*, not what was measured. In a 2025 OECD survey of ten countries, 91% of SMEs using generative AI reported efficiency gains; the figure reaches us through a blog by a PayPal executive, and PayPal was a survey partner ([Nash, OECD blog, 2025](https://oecdcogito.blog/2025/09/16/agentic-ai-for-small-business-growth/)). The OECD's 2026 report names time, maintenance costs and skills as what holds SMEs back ([OECD, 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/empowering-smes-in-the-age-of-ai_bf5a9816-en.html)).

For the smallest firms the brake is knowledge, not money. Of Dutch microbusinesses that considered AI and decided against it, 72% named lack of experience, 49% privacy and 43% legal consequences; 20% said it cost too much ([CBS, 2026](https://www.cbs.nl/nl-nl/longread/rapportages/2026/gebruik-van-ai-technologie-door-nederlandse-microbedrijven/6-redenen-geen-ai-technologie)). That is the gap page 1 describes.

We found no independent Dutch measurement of payback for a firm of 2–75 people. So the honest answer is: **measure it in your own firm**, on a use small enough to measure.

### Why we chose invoice processing

- **It answers the gap from page 1.** Even the smallest firm has the numbers to judge it: how many invoices arrive, and how long each takes.
- **It is low-risk.** The AI does the routine part, reading and preparing; it only advises on the booking. A person approves every booking before anything is paid.
- **It is ready-made.** It often sits in the accounting software the firm already pays for, sometimes in a plan of about €15 a month (section 4).
- **It fits both sizes, with one difference.** A firm of 30 saves staff hours. A firm of two saves fewer hours, but they are the owner's own, often evening hours, so the cheapest route counts most (section 3).
- **The catch.** The Dutch government plans structured e-invoices (Peppol) for domestic B2B from 2030, though these dates are still planning scenarios, not law ([Banqup, 2026](https://www.banqup.com/resources/blog/netherlands-peppol-based-vida-rollout-including-domestic-b2b-mandate-by-2027)). Structured invoices need no AI to read them. So avoid a long contract for PDF-reading alone.

## 3. Illustrative use case: incoming purchase invoices, read by AI

The firm lets its accounting software read incoming invoices and suggest the booking; a person checks and approves each one. It runs a pilot of four to six weeks and keeps it only if the hours saved are worth more than the tool costs. Below, the six CRISP-DM phases, with the firm's real data situation stated plainly.

1. **Business understanding.** Goal: less time and fewer errors in booking purchase invoices, without losing control over payments. Decide the success measures *before* starting: minutes per invoice, share of invoices that need correcting, and days from receipt to approval. Agree the decision rule up front: continue only if the monthly saving beats the monthly cost.
2. **Data understanding.** What the firm really has: PDF invoices in a mailbox, some on paper, a supplier list in the accounting package, and past bookings with their ledger codes. Those past bookings are the only "training data" it owns. The reality is messy: poor scans, one supplier under three names, and tens of invoices a month in a firm of two, a few hundred in a firm of 30 — never thousands.
3. **Data preparation.** Open one dedicated e-mail address for invoices only. Clean the supplier list and merge duplicates. Agree which ledger codes are used for what. Ask the largest suppliers to send e-invoices.
4. **Modelling.** The firm builds no model. It switches on and configures a ready-made feature: invoice scanning and recognition, an approval step with amount limits, and recurring bookings for fixed costs. The settings *are* the model, so write them down.
5. **Evaluation.** Run the pilot beside the old way for four to six weeks. Each week, check AI-suggested bookings by hand — all of them in a small firm, a sample of 20 in a larger one — and count the errors. Compare minutes per invoice with the baseline. Then decide: scale up, adjust, or stop.
6. **Deployment.** Write the new routine on one page: who approves what up to which amount, who checks the bookings, and what may and may not go into AI tools. In a firm of two, that page is a short agreement between the two people. Review the numbers every quarter.

### Is it worth it? The sum the owner makes

```
monthly gain     = (minutes before − minutes after) × invoices per month ÷ 60 × hourly cost − monthly tool cost
payback (months) = one-off setup cost ÷ monthly gain
```

*Example numbers, not data* — both firms go from 5 to 2 minutes per invoice, valued at €40 an hour:

| Firm | Invoices a month | Hours saved a month | Value before tool cost |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Two people | 40 | 2 | €80 |
| 30 people | 250 | 12.5 | €500 |

Set that against the tool's price: from about €15 a month for a small-firm package to €49–€299 for larger SME plans (section 4). For a firm of two, the sum only works if the feature is in a cheap plan or one it already pays for; fewer errors and free evenings count too, but cannot be put in euros. Put in your own counts and your supplier's quote; if the monthly gain is zero or less, stop.

## 4. Recommended tooling

Start with what the firm already pays for; buy something new only if that falls short. Prices change, so ask your supplier for a quote in writing.

| Option | What it is for | Effort | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The AI invoice feature in your own accounting package | Reads invoices, suggests bookings, routes approval | Low: a setup session and a cleaned supplier list | Ask your supplier. Example: Exact lists invoice scanning, Peppol receipt and approval flows of up to five steps, with a half-day online setup by a consultant ([Exact, factsheet](https://files.exact.com/static/web/downloads/NL-SME-Getting-started-Automatische-Factuurverwerking.pdf)); its SME plans started at €49–€299 a month in 2026 ([Onderneming.nl, 2026](https://www.onderneming.nl/boekhoudprogramma/exact/)) |
| A small-firm accounting package | The same job at small-firm prices, for firms of one to a few people | Low: the owner can set it up in an afternoon | Example: Moneybird lists "slimme documenten" — 3 a month on its Compact plan, unlimited from its Start plan at €15–€18 a month ([Onderneming.nl, 2026](https://www.onderneming.nl/boekhoudprogramma/moneybird/)). Ask whether that includes automatic recognition |
| E-invoice receipt via Peppol | Invoices arrive as data, so nothing needs reading | Low per supplier; needs suppliers to join | Check whether your plan includes it |
| A general AI chatbot | Drafting e-mails, explaining a ledger code | None to start | Free tiers exist — **not for invoices**: they hold names and bank details (section 5) |

Whatever you pick, ask the supplier three things: where is our data processed, is it used to train their models, and can we get it all back if we leave?

## 5. Responsible AI and trust

The AI suggests; a person decides. Nothing is paid on an AI suggestion alone.

- **When may the AI act, and when only advise?** It may do the routine, low-risk part on its own: read the invoice and prepare the booking. Anything that needs judgement — approving, paying, changing a supplier's bank details — stays with a person.
- **Does the firm's data stay its own?** Invoices carry names, addresses and bank details, often of sole traders, so they are personal data under the GDPR. Use the feature inside your accounting software under its processing agreement, not a free chatbot. The Dutch Data Protection Authority (AP) received dozens of reports in 2024 and 2025 of sensitive data being pasted into AI chatbots, and advises clear rules on what may be entered ([Accountant.nl on AP, 2025](https://www.accountant.nl/nieuws/2025/12/ap-slaat-alarm-over-datalekken-door-ai/)).
- **AI nobody mentions.** In a firm of 30, staff may use free AI tools without saying so. In a firm of two, that is often the owner, pasting an invoice into a chatbot to save a minute. Either way, client data leaves the firm and nobody checks the answer. A one-page rule on what may go into AI tools covers both.
- **How far can the output be trusted?** Only as far as your own checks show. Hand-checked bookings tell the owner the real error rate; a supplier's brochure does not.
- **The law on skills.** The EU AI Act asks firms that use AI to take measures for the AI literacy of the people who use it. A 2026 amendment softened the wording, but the duty for users stays ([KI-Campus, 2026](https://ki-campus.org/en/blog/eu-ai-act-ai-omnibus-recalibrates-article-4-ai-literacy-remains-mandatory)). For this use, a short briefing and the one-page routine are a sensible start.
- **Guardrails.** Approval limits by amount, a second approver above a set limit where there are two people, no automatic payment, and the regular checks.
- **The people doing the work.** In a firm of two, the time saved goes back to the owner's own work. In a larger firm, the person who now types invoices will spend time on checking and odd cases instead. Involve them from day one: they know which suppliers cause trouble. Do not promise or plan job cuts on pilot numbers.

## 6. First five steps for an owner

Start on Monday by counting, not buying.

1. **Count for one week.** If fewer than 20 invoices arrive a week, count for a month. Tally every incoming purchase invoice and the minutes each takes, from arrival to approval. Note every correction.
2. **Ask your software supplier** what AI invoice reading your current plan offers, what it costs, where the data is processed, and whether it is used for training.
3. **Prepare the ground.** Open one invoice-only mailbox, clean the supplier list, and ask your five biggest suppliers for e-invoices.
4. **Pilot for four to six weeks** with a person approving every booking and the AI's suggestions checked by hand each week.
5. **Decide with the sum from section 3,** and write the one-page routine, including what staff may put into AI tools.

### How to tell it works

- Minutes per invoice drop clearly below your week-one baseline.
- Your weekly check shows few errors, and the number falls rather than rises.
- Invoices reach approval sooner, and none goes missing or is paid twice.

## What this page cannot tell you yet — and what our interview will add

Without the interview, this page cannot say how many invoices a real firm of this kind handles, how long they take, which software it uses, or whether AI is already used without anyone saying so. Those four facts decide the business case. National figures show what is common, not what one firm does, and the payback figures we found are self-reported.

Our interview guide (ten questions, 45–60 minutes) is written for a two-person business-service firm. It asks about real workflows first and about AI only later, so the answers do not depend on how much the owner already knows about AI. Each gap on this page has its question:

**Our rule for the answers.** If Q2 and Q4 point to another task than purchase invoices, we take that task through the same six phases, the same sum and the same five steps. The use case on this page is our best guess from desk research, not yet the firm's choice.

One limit stays after the interview: a firm of two is not a firm of 75. Where the answers would differ by size, the page will say so.
